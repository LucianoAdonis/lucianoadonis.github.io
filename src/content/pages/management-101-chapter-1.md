## Why Someone Would Choose This?

I like to think of it as a specialization in your skill tree. If you are like me, you probably have a lot of fun wrestling with code and devs, which leads you to a different specialization, the Subject Matter Expert (SME).

But I chose the Manager Life when planning my career next steps, even though most would assume the obvious move was to double down on what I was already good at.
Personal experience led me down this path. The reason? Like playing with LEGO, as an SME, you get to design, plan, and build whatever is needed. And that's fun, competing to create better and faster? That's my jam! But... I try to be efficient with my time and above all else, my life.

I've met great managers who saved me, and our clients, a ton of wasted effort. Chasing the newest tech just for the sake of it? Not always the best play.

So, I'd rather fight smarter... even if that means dealing with more people than machines.

Managing doesn't replace your technical skills, it sits on top of them. The people who struggle most early on are the ones who try to stop being technical altogether, and <span data-review="new">I think</span> that's a bad move.

<!-- source: 2026 batch (8126bde), restored -->
What changes is where your time goes: you build less yourself and spend more of it unblocking others.

### Communication

<!-- source: 2026 batch (8126bde), restored -->
It's the most important one, and the one most engineers underestimate. I'm talking about saying what you mean to someone who doesn't share your context, and understanding what they mean when they say something vague, rather than presentations or writing reports.

<!-- source: 2026 batch (8126bde), restored -->
Most of the miscommunication in engineering teams has nothing technical in it: a project manager and a developer using the word "done" for two different things.

### Prioritization

<!-- source: 2026 batch (8126bde), restored -->
You'll always have more to do than time allows, so the job is deciding what doesn't get done today, and being able to explain that decision to your team and to your manager.

<!-- source: 2026 batch (8126bde), restored -->
It's harder than it sounds, <span data-review="new">mostly because a good call rarely gets noticed.</span>

### People Sense

<!-- source: 2026 batch (8126bde), restored -->
You need to read what motivates someone, notice when they're struggling but not saying it, and guess when feedback will land or backfire. <span data-review="new">I'd say it's</span> part intuition, part paying attention.

<!-- source: 2026 batch (8126bde), restored -->
You won't get it right every time. Aim to be wrong less often than you'd be if you ignored it.

### Running Meetings

<!-- source: 2026 batch (8126bde), restored -->
Most meetings are bad and most engineers know it, so this one gets its own name. A good meeting has a clear purpose, starts on time, ends with a decision or a next action, and leaves out the people who have nothing to do there.

<!-- source: 2026 batch (8126bde), restored -->
If you can't describe in one sentence what the meeting is for, you're not ready to schedule it.
