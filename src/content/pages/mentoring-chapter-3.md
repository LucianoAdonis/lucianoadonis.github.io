## When Mentoring Fails

Not every mentoring relationship works. Some fail quietly: the meetings get further apart, the messages stop, and both sides move on without admitting it ended. Others fail loudly, with a disagreement, a misalignment, or the realization that the relationship is doing more harm than good.

That's normal. Mentoring is a human relationship, and human relationships have failure modes.

### Mismatched Expectations

The most common failure. The mentee expects a career coach, the mentor expects a curious learner, and nobody said what they wanted, so both are disappointed.

**The fix is obvious:** talk about expectations up front. What does the mentee want out of this? What can the mentor realistically provide? How often will you meet? What topics are in scope?

**Why people skip it:** it feels too formal for a relationship that's supposed to be organic. But a five-minute conversation about expectations saves months of frustration.

### The Mentee Who Doesn't Follow Through

You give them a book to read and they don't read it. You suggest they lead a retro and they don't volunteer. You give feedback on their code and the patterns you discussed don't change.

Before you decide they're not serious, ask yourself a few things. Are they overwhelmed? Are they afraid of failing at the thing you suggested? Is the advice relevant to where they are right now, or to where you think they should be?

If they really aren't invested, it's okay to say so. "I've noticed we aren't making progress on the things we discuss. Is this still useful to you?" Give them an honest exit.

### The Mentor Who Overinvests

This one is about you. Some mentors attach their identity to their mentee's success. When the mentee thrives, the mentor feels validated, and when the mentee struggles or leaves, the mentor feels like they failed.

Your mentee's career isn't your report card. You can give guidance, create opportunities, and offer support, but you can't control outcomes. If they leave for a better offer or pick a path you didn't recommend, they're making their own decisions, which is literally the point of mentoring.

## When to Let Go

A few signals that a mentoring relationship has run its course:

- **The conversations are repetitive.** You keep covering the same ground without progress. Usually the person needs something you can't provide, maybe a different perspective, maybe professional help, maybe just time.
- **The mentee has outgrown you.** This is the best possible outcome. If they're asking questions you can't answer, introduce them to someone who can. Your job is to be the right mentor for this stage, not the last one.
- **It's costing you too much.** Mentoring takes energy. If you're neglecting your own work, growth, or well-being because of it, the relationship isn't sustainable.
- **The trust is gone.** If the mentee stops being honest with you, or you stop being honest with them, the relationship has lost its foundation.

## The Leadership Question

Oh, it depends on how you look at it. For some it may be saying the right thing, while for others it may be doing the right thing. You won't make everyone happy, so you may as well be happy and proud of yourself.

Leadership through mentoring is quiet: no title, nothing in your performance review.

Say the person you mentored three years ago makes a great decision today. Nobody connects it back to you, and that's how the return on mentoring works: invisible and delayed.

### Leading by Example

People learn more from how you behave than from your advice: how you handle pressure, respond to failure, treat people who can't do anything for you, and admit when you're wrong.

People watch before they listen. If your actions contradict your advice, they'll follow the actions.

### The Ripple Effect

Most of the impact lands on the people your mentee goes on to mentor. If you teach someone to give honest feedback, they'll give honest feedback to others. If you teach someone to create safe failures, they'll do the same for the next person, and you're influencing a chain of people you'll never meet.

So yes, mentoring is leadership. It just doesn't come with a salary bump or applause.

You do it because you remember what it was like when someone did it for you, or because you remember what it was like when nobody did.
