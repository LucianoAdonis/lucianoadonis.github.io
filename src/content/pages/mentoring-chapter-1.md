## Three Different Things

People use "mentoring," "managing," and "teaching" interchangeably. They aren't the same thing, and confusing them is how most mentoring relationships fail.

**Managing**: you're on the hook for outcomes. You need this done by Friday. Here are the priorities. Let me know if you're blocked. The relationship is structural, it exists because the org chart says so. A manager makes sure the work gets done and the person doing it has what they need.

**Teaching**: you're passing on knowledge. Here's how this works. Here's why it works that way. Let me show you. The relationship is transactional: I have the information, you need it, and we meet until you have it.

**Mentoring**: you're helping someone grow. It's less structured than managing and less transactional than teaching. A mentor isn't responsible for your output or your curriculum. A mentor helps you figure out which questions to ask, which paths to consider, and which mistakes are worth making.

It matters because if you approach mentoring like managing, you end up micromanaging someone's career. If you approach it like teaching, you end up lecturing.

## What a Mentor Actually Does

Strip away the inspirational language and a mentor does a few specific things.

### Shares Context

What a mentor mostly brings is context. They've been in the situation you're in now. They know what it looks like from the other side, and which battles are worth fighting and which are decorative.

"You're stressing about this code review, but the issue is that you haven't aligned with the tech lead on the approach. Fix that first, and the review will be easy."

### Asks Questions Instead of Giving Answers

A good mentor resists the urge to just tell you the answer. It can look like withholding, but working it out yourself is what sticks.

"What do you think would happen if you deployed that change to staging first?"

They already know the answer. But if they say "deploy to staging first," you learn one step. If they ask the question, you learn to think about deployment risk for every future change.

### Creates Safe Failures

Growth needs failure, the controlled kind. A mentor creates space where someone can try, mess up, and learn without the consequences being career-ending.

That might mean letting a junior engineer lead a sprint planning session, knowing it will be messy. Or letting someone debug an issue for longer than it would take you to fix it, because the debugging is the lesson.

Probably the hardest part of mentoring is watching someone struggle with something you could solve in five minutes, and deciding their struggle is worth more than your efficiency.

### Gives Honest Feedback

Honest, which usually lands somewhere between harsh and sugar-coated.

"Your presentation had good content, but you lost the room in the middle. You went too deep on the technical details when the audience needed the business impact. Next time, lead with what it means for them."

Most people don't get this kind of feedback. They get "great job" or silence, and neither helps them grow. A mentor is often the only person willing to say the specific, uncomfortable, useful thing.

## The Informal Mentor

Most mentoring relationships never get formalized. Nobody signed a document and there's no schedule. Someone started asking you for advice, you started giving it, and over time both of you came to rely on it.

These informal relationships are often better than formal programs because people pick their own mentors. They chose you because something about how you work or think resonates with them, which is a better foundation than an HR matching algorithm.

If you're in an informal mentoring role, remember one thing: you accepted it by engaging. You don't need a title to take it seriously.
