## Stages

<!-- source: drafts/mentoring.md -->
Sometimes the difference in some aspects of life will be noticeable and you'll have to adjust to the rhythm <span data-review="new">the person you're mentoring</span> moves to.

From my personal experience it's more like "Oh, I was like that at X years old." That recognition is useful, but only if you resist the urge to skip them past the stage they're in. Everyone has to live through their own version of it.

### The Eager Beginner

Everything is new and exciting. They want to learn everything at once and volunteer for things they don't fully understand. Energy is high, direction is low.

**What they need:** structure, small wins, and protection from overcommitting. Give them clear tasks with defined boundaries. Celebrate the first thing they ship. Don't let them take on three projects to prove themselves.

**Common mistake:** assuming their enthusiasm means they can handle ambiguity. They can't. They're eager because they don't know enough to be overwhelmed yet, and that changes fast if you throw them into the deep end.

### The Frustrated Intermediate

They know enough to see how much they don't know. The initial excitement has worn off. They can do the work, but they question whether they're doing it well enough. Impostor syndrome lives here.

**What they need:** validation that the frustration is normal, exposure to the bigger picture, and harder problems. This is where a mentor's context matters most. "I felt exactly this way two years in. It passes. Here's what helped me."

**Common mistake:** more technical training. At this stage it's usually confidence and perspective. Another tutorial won't fix "I don't feel like I belong here."

### The Quiet Competent

They can do most things on their own and rarely ask for help, because they don't need it day to day. They might seem like they don't need a mentor anymore.

They do.

**What they need:** challenge. New dimensions of growth beyond their comfort zone, like leadership opportunities, cross-team exposure, or a project outside their specialty. And someone to bounce ideas off when the stakes get higher.

**Common mistake:** ignoring them because they're "fine." The quiet competent is the person most likely to leave without warning, because nobody noticed they'd outgrown the role.

### The Experienced Peer

At some point the person you're mentoring catches up. They might even pass you in some areas. The relationship shifts from guidance to swapping perspectives.

**What they need:** you to acknowledge the shift. The worst thing a mentor can do here is keep acting like the authority. The best is to say "I think you know more about this than I do. What do you think?"

This is also where mentoring goes both ways. You start learning from them, and if that bruises your ego, mentoring isn't for you.

## Different People, Different Approaches

Experience level is one axis. Personality is another.

### The Talker

Processes everything out loud and needs to verbalize before deciding. Meetings with them run long. Slack messages are paragraphs.

**Approach:** let them talk, but help them find the signal in their own noise. After they explain their thinking, ask: "If you had to summarize that in one sentence, what would it be?" You're not dismissing them, you're teaching them to find the core of their own thoughts.

### The Silent One

Processes internally and answers questions with three words. You're never sure if they're stuck, thinking, or bored.

**Approach:** don't fill the silence. Ask a question and wait. If they need time, give them time. Written communication (async messages, shared docs) often works better than live conversation. They're engaged, just quietly.

### The Perfectionist

Won't ship until it's flawless. Every code review has 40 comments and every document goes through five drafts. Quality is high, velocity is low.

**Approach:** help them find the line between "good enough" and "perfect." Timeboxing is your friend: "Spend two hours on this and show me what you have." Show them real examples of shipped work that wasn't perfect and succeeded anyway.

### The Speedrunner

Ships fast, breaks things, moves on. Velocity is high, quality varies. They'd rather ask forgiveness than permission.

**Approach:** redirect the speed instead of braking it. Point it at things where fast iteration pays off (prototypes, spikes, proofs of concept). For things that need precision, pair with them so they learn to match speed to stakes.

---

## The One Rule

Every person is a different mix of stage, personality, context, and goals, so there's no universal approach to mentoring.

There is one rule that applies to all of them, though: **meet them where they are, not where you think they should be.**

The eager beginner doesn't need your senior-level take on architecture. The frustrated intermediate doesn't need a pep talk about potential. The quiet competent needs harder problems.
